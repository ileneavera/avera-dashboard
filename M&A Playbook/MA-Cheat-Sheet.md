## 1. Term Sheet Anatomy — Know These Cold

A term sheet is non-binding on most things but sets the blueprint. Everything negotiated in the definitive docs traces back to what was agreed (or left ambiguous) in the TS. Sellers often don't realize how much leverage they have *before* signing exclusivity — and how little they have after.

### Purchase Price & Consideration
- **All cash at close** = cleanest, lowest risk for seller
- **Stock component** = seller is betting on the acquirer's future; understand liquidity, lockup, registration rights before accepting
- **Earnout** = deferred consideration tied to future milestones; highest risk (see Section 3)
- **The headline number isn't the real number.** A $20M offer with a $10M earnout is very different from a $20M all-cash offer. Always normalize for structure.

### Escrow / Holdback
- **Market:** 10–15% of purchase price held for 12–18 months to cover indemnification claims
- **Red flags:** >15% amount, >18 months duration, overly broad triggers for claims
- **Push for:** 10% amount, 12-month duration, narrow and specific claim triggers

### Representations & Warranties
- Seller represents that everything stated about the business is true. If not → buyer makes indemnification claims against escrow (or beyond)
- **Key variables:**
  - *Survival period* — how long after closing can buyer bring claims? (market: 12–18 months)
  - *Cap* — max total liability, usually equals escrow amount (market: 10–15% of deal value)
  - *Basket/deductible* — buyer must exceed a threshold before claiming (market: 0.5–1% of deal value)
- **R&W Insurance:** Lets buyer claim against an insurer rather than seller escrow. Increasingly common, generally seller-friendly. Worth raising.
- **Where surprises live:** Messy contracts, unclear IP ownership, inconsistent financials, unrecorded liabilities. These surface in diligence and come back as R&W claims.

### Working Capital Adjustment
- Deal includes a target WC peg (expected level of cash, AR, AP at close)
- Deliver more than target → seller gets more. Deliver less → buyer claws back.
- **Common post-close dispute** — especially for companies that didn't model it carefully
- **Push for:** Peg based on trailing 12-month average, not an arbitrary buyer-set number

### Exclusivity
- Once signed, seller cannot engage other buyers for the exclusivity period
- **Market:** 30–60 days, sometimes with one buyer extension right
- **This is when leverage disappears.** Everything not locked in the TS becomes very hard to fix later. Treat exclusivity signing as a major threshold.
- **Push for:** 45 days max, reciprocal good-faith obligation from buyer, right to terminate if buyer goes quiet

### Employee Matters
- **Option treatment:** Do unvested options accelerate at close? Fully, partially, or not at all? Critical for employee morale and retention.
- **Key employee retention:** Buyers often condition close on signed offer letters from key employees. Are retention bonuses in the deal? Funded by buyer or from proceeds?
- **Non-competes:** Duration (market: 2 years), geography, scope — especially important for founders
- **Employment agreements:** Founders/executives often transition from equity owners to employees. What are the termination terms? "Without cause" protection?

### Conditions to Closing
- List of things that must happen before the deal closes: regulatory approvals, third-party consents, no MAC, key employee offers
- **The longer the list, the more execution risk for the seller**
- Common in SaaS/tech: customer contract anti-assignment provisions (see Section 5)

### Break Fees & Reverse Termination Fees
- If seller walks → buyer typically gets 1–3% of deal value
- If buyer walks → seller often gets nothing (especially in small deals)
- **Pushing for a reverse termination fee** is a sign of a sophisticated seller — protects against buyer doing 60 days of diligence and walking away

## 2. Sell-Side Process Quality — Questions to Ask Any Client

When a company comes in with term sheets in hand, the quality of the process they ran determines how much leverage they actually have.

**Did they run a real competitive process?**
- Multiple buyers actively bidding vs. one inbound that turned into a negotiation
- Even one credible competing offer creates significant leverage on price and terms
- If it was mostly one buyer: they may have anchored too early and not pushed hard enough

**Is the data room clean?**
- Organized financial model, customer contracts, IP assignments, cap table, board minutes, employee agreements
- A messy data room signals immaturity → buyer uses it to re-trade on price or load up on indemnities
- A clean data room says "well-run company" — affects perception as much as the numbers
- **Ilene's service:** Audit the data room before diligence kicks off, identify gaps, help them present clean

**Did they disclose too much too early?**
- Pre-NDA conversations and early buyer meetings often involve oversharing
- If a buyer knows about churn problems, pipeline gaps, or product weaknesses — they'll use it
- Can't undo, but need to inventory what's out there before negotiations escalate

**Is the board/investor base aligned?**
- Preferred stockholders (investors) have liquidation preferences and participation rights
- Do all investors agree on: minimum acceptable price? Timing? This specific buyer?
- A misaligned board going into final negotiations is a serious execution risk

**Do they have real M&A counsel?**
- Corporate lawyers ≠ M&A lawyers. The other side almost certainly has specialists.
- Using general outside counsel for an acquisition means getting outlawyered
- Ask who their counsel is and whether they've done M&A before

## 3. Earnouts — The #1 Landmine for Sellers

Earnouts are the most common way small company sellers get hurt. They look like more money. They often aren't.

### How They Work
Buyer offers: "We'll pay $15M at close + up to $5M more if you hit $X revenue over 2 years." The $5M is the earnout.

### Why They're Dangerous
- **The buyer controls the business post-close.** Headcount, pricing, sales investment, product direction — if they want to manage toward or away from the earnout target, they largely can.
- **Integration kills standalone revenue.** Once absorbed into a larger product, the target metric may become impossible.
- **Metric definitions matter enormously.** "Revenue" sounds simple: GAAP? ARR? Bookings? Renewals? Gross vs. net? Small differences in definition = large differences in payout.
- **Disputes are expensive.** Earnout litigation is common; the small company almost always loses simply because it can't afford to fight.

### If an Earnout Is Unavoidable — Push For:
- **Short duration** (1 year, not 2–3)
- **Metrics seller has direct line of sight to** (customer count or NRR rather than EBITDA, which buyer controls via G&A allocation)
- **Acceleration on change of control** — if the acquirer is itself acquired, earnout pays out in full immediately
- **Operational protections** — buyer can't reduce headcount below X, can't change pricing without notice, must maintain product investment at $Y
- **A floor** — minimum earnout payout regardless of performance
- **An accelerator** — if they exceed the target, payout scales up beyond the cap

### Seasonality Watch
For seasonal businesses (EdTech, retail, tax software, etc.): make sure earnout measurement periods account for seasonality. A February snapshot of a business with summer-heavy renewals will always look bad.

## 4. Strategic vs. Financial Buyer — Different Dynamics

| | Strategic Buyer | Financial Buyer (PE/Growth Equity) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Product fit, customers, team, technology | EBITDA, growth rate, customer retention |
| Valuation basis | Synergies justify higher price | Multiple of revenue or EBITDA |
| Earnout risk | High — integration complicates measurement | High — EBITDA controlled via cost allocation |
| Non-competes | Often tighter, especially for founders | Standard |
| Post-close structure | Absorption into larger entity | Often keeps standalone + requires mgmt rollover |
| Key question to ask | What happens to the product/brand/team? | What is mgmt's role and equity stake post-close? |
| Financing risk | Lower (strategic uses own capital) | Higher if PE uses debt financing |

**One thing strategics almost never tell you:** How quickly they'll integrate. The answer matters enormously for earnout achievability.

## 5. Sector-Specific Notes

### SaaS / Subscription Software
- Diligence focuses on: ARR, NRR, churn, CAC, LTV, cohort retention
- Customer contract quality: multi-year vs. annual, auto-renewal vs. manual
- Revenue recognition matters: when is it actually earned? Watch for deferred revenue treatment.
- Change-of-control provisions in enterprise contracts

### EdTech
- **Customer contract assignability:** School district and university contracts often require explicit customer consent before a deal can close. Number of affected contracts and ease of getting consent can determine deal timing.
- **Student data privacy:** FERPA (all ages) and COPPA (under 13) create specific R&W exposure. Buyers will probe hard. Any past incidents = major indemnification risk.
- **LMS integration agreements:** Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard, etc. often have partner/API agreements with change-of-control provisions. Buyer must be able to maintain access.
- **Revenue seasonality:** Renewal cycles tied to school year (summer/fall heavy). Measurement periods must account for this.
- **Government procurement rules:** Some district contracts have transfer restrictions under state/local procurement law.

## 6. Market Benchmarks — Quick Reference

| Term | Seller-Friendly | Market Norm | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Escrow amount | 8% | 10–12% | >15% |
| Escrow duration | 12 months | 12–18 months | 24+ months |
| R&W survival period | 12 months | 12–18 months | 24+ months |
| Indemnification cap | 8–10% of deal | = escrow | Uncapped or >25% |
| Basket / deductible | 1% of deal | 0.5–1% | Very low (buyer claims freely) |
| Exclusivity period | 30 days | 45 days | 60–90 days |
| Earnout duration | 1 year | 1–2 years | 3 years |
| Break fee (if seller walks) | <1% | 1–3% of deal | >3% |
| Non-compete duration | 18 months | 2 years | 3+ years |

## 8. One-Liners to Have Ready

*"How do you think about evaluating an earnout?"*
→ "I look at three things: what metric it's tied to, who controls the business post-close, and whether there are operational protections. If the buyer controls the inputs that drive the metric, the earnout isn't really consideration — it's a hope."

*"What's the most common mistake at this stage?"*
→ "Losing leverage before you realize it. Companies sign exclusivity before locking down key economic terms, and then everything becomes a negotiation from a position of weakness. Get as much agreed in the TS as possible."

*"Should we take the highest offer?"*
→ "Not always. Structure matters as much as number. A higher headline with a big earnout and aggressive escrow can net out lower than a smaller clean all-cash deal. And buyer quality matters — can they close? Will they treat the team well? Those affect earnout risk and employee retention."

*"What does a data room problem look like in practice?"*
→ "Missing IP assignments, unsigned customer contracts, inconsistent financials between the model and the actuals. Buyers use those gaps to re-trade on price or load up indemnities. A clean data room says 'well-run company' — it affects perception as much as the numbers."

*"Why do you need someone like Avera if you already have M&A lawyers?"*
→ "Lawyers handle the legal documents. They don't tell you what to push back on, how to read the buyer's psychology, whether the earnout structure is realistic, or how to keep the board aligned. That's the gap I fill — the strategic operator layer between the legal work and the business decision."
