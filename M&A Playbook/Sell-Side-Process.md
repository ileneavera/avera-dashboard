# Sell-Side Process
### Avera M&A Playbook

A full walkthrough of running a sell-side M&A process — from the decision to sell through close. Written from the perspective of a fractional corp dev advisor embedded with a founder/CEO team.

*Last updated: March 2026*

---

## Overview: The Full Arc

```
Phase 1: Readiness        → Get the house in order before anyone sees it
Phase 2: Preparation      → Build materials and data room
Phase 3: Go-to-Market     → Identify buyers, distribute teasers, get NDAs signed
Phase 4: First Round      → IOIs / indications of interest, select who advances
Phase 5: Second Round     → Full data room, management presentations, final bids
Phase 6: Term Sheet       → Select preferred buyer, negotiate TS, sign exclusivity
Phase 7: Diligence        → Manage the process, keep momentum, avoid re-trades
Phase 8: Definitive Docs  → Work with counsel to sign and close
```

Timeline: A well-run process typically takes **4–6 months** from launch to close. Compressed processes (1 buyer, inbound) can close in 2–3 months but usually leave value on the table.

---

## Phase 1: Readiness — Before You Start

The biggest mistake companies make: going to market before they're ready. One bad first impression with a buyer can't be undone.

### Board & Investor Alignment
- Before doing anything, all major shareholders need to agree on: Are we selling? At what minimum price? On what timeline? To what type of buyer?
- Investor misalignment discovered mid-process is a deal killer. Surface it early.
- Map the cap table: who has blocking rights? Who has information rights? Who needs to approve?
- Understand the preference stack: what do preferred shareholders get vs. common? At what price does everyone get paid?

### Strategic Clarity on Goals
Have the founder/CEO articulate clearly (even if just internally):
- **Why selling now?** (Board pressure, burn, strategic fit, founder fatigue — it affects how you run the process)
- **What's the minimum acceptable price?**
- **What matters beyond price?** (Team retention, product continuity, brand, customer treatment)
- **Strategic vs. financial buyer preference?** (And why)
- **Timeline constraints?** (Runway, contract renewals, competitive dynamics)

### Legal & Financial Hygiene
Run a pre-diligence audit before buyers see anything. Common issues:
- IP assignments not signed by all employees/contractors (especially early ones)
- Customer contracts with unsigned amendments or side letters
- Employee agreements missing (NDAs, invention assignment, non-solicits)
- Cap table discrepancies between equity management platform and corporate records
- Unpaid or unaccrued liabilities, deferred revenue treatment
- Board minutes missing for key decisions
- Any ongoing litigation or regulatory issues that need to be understood and framed

**Ilene's role:** Walk through the diligence checklist, identify gaps, help prioritize what to fix before launch vs. what to disclose cleanly.

## Phase 2: Preparation — Building the Materials

### The Data Room
Set up before any buyer gets access. Standard structure:

```
1. Corporate
   - Certificate of incorporation, bylaws, amendments
   - Board minutes (last 3 years)
   - Cap table (fully diluted) + equity agreements
   - Subsidiary structure (if any)

2. Financials
   - Audited financials or reviewed financials (last 3 years)
   - Monthly P&L, balance sheet, cash flow (last 2 years + YTD)
   - Financial model with projections (3–5 years)
   - Revenue bridge (ARR waterfall for SaaS)
   - Customer cohort analysis

3. Commercial
   - Top 20 customer contracts (redacted for initial access)
   - Customer list with ARR, start date, renewal date, status
   - Standard MSA / subscription agreement
   - Pipeline and forecast

4. Employees
   - Org chart
   - Offer letters for key employees
   - Equity grants by employee
   - Benefits overview
   - Any severance agreements

5. IP & Technology
   - IP ownership assignments
   - Patent filings (if any)
   - Open source usage and licenses
   - Key vendor/technology agreements

6. Legal
   - Material contracts (partnerships, reseller agreements, data agreements)
   - Any litigation, claims, or regulatory matters
   - Insurance policies

7. Real Estate
   - Office leases (if any)
```

**Pro tip:** Organize so that a first-time viewer can navigate without asking questions. Every question a buyer asks = a signal of incomplete information.

### The Teaser (2 pages, anonymous)
- Overview of the business: what it does, who the customers are, key metrics
- Financial snapshot: revenue, growth rate, margin
- Why it's being sold (brief, framed positively)
- What the buyer opportunity looks like
- **No company name, no identifying details** — sent before NDA is signed

### The CIM (Confidential Information Memorandum)
Full marketing document, sent post-NDA. Typically 25–50 pages covering:
1. Executive summary / investment thesis
2. Company overview and history
3. Product/technology overview
4. Market opportunity
5. Go-to-market and customer overview
6. Financial performance and projections
7. Team bios
8. Transaction overview (what's being offered)

**Tips for small companies:**
- Keep it focused. Buyers skim. Lead with the most compelling metrics.
- Don't oversell projections — buyers will diligence them and losing credibility on the model is a deal risk
- Have a clear "why buy this company" narrative. If you can't articulate it, the buyer won't either.

### The Financial Model
- Should tell a clean story: here's where we've been, here's where we're going, here's why
- Key SaaS metrics front and center: ARR, NRR, Gross Margin, CAC, LTV, Churn
- Three scenarios (base, upside, downside) — shows sophistication and intellectual honesty
- Be able to defend every assumption — buyers will probe

### Management Presentation
- Used in second-round management meetings (in-person or video)
- Typically 45–60 minutes: 30–40 min presentation, 20–30 min Q&A
- Cover: company story, product demo (optional), market, financials, team, why now, what we're looking for in a partner
- **Practice the hard questions** — see below

## Phase 3: Go-to-Market — Identifying Buyers and Running Outreach

### Building the Buyer Universe
Start broad, then tier. Categories to think through:

**Strategic buyers:**
- Direct competitors (want to acquire customers/revenue; tricky culturally)
- Adjacent product companies (want to expand into your category)
- Platform companies where your product becomes a feature
- Distribution companies (want to add your product to their offering)
- Companies where your tech/team is the primary appeal (acqui-hire)

**Financial buyers:**
- PE firms with relevant sector expertise
- Growth equity firms at the right stage
- Search funds (for smaller deals, founder-led)
- Family offices with relevant holdings

**Tiering the list:**
- **Tier 1:** High strategic fit, likely interested, likely can close → contact first
- **Tier 2:** Good fit but less obvious motivation or more complex process
- **Tier 3:** Long shots — worth sending teaser but don't prioritize

**For small companies:** The buyer universe is often smaller than expected. 20–40 serious outreaches is common. Not 200.

### Running Outreach
- Teaser goes out with a brief email: "We're running a confidential process for a company in X space. Given your portfolio/focus, thought you might be interested. Happy to share more under NDA."
- Track responses in a process tracker (who has NDA, who's passed, who's engaged)
- Follow up once. If no response, that's a pass.
- Don't burn relationships by pushing too hard on passes — buyers talk to each other

### NDAs
- Get NDAs signed before sending the CIM
- Watch for: overly broad non-solicits (restricts you from hiring their employees post-process), standstill provisions, long duration
- Use a standard seller-form NDA if possible — easier to track deviations

## Phase 4: First Round — IOIs

### What to Ask For in an IOI
- Indicative valuation range (or single number) — be explicit about what's included (cash-free, debt-free basis)
- Deal structure (all cash, stock, earnout, rollover)
- Key conditions or assumptions
- Proposed timeline
- Brief description of why they're interested and their integration thesis

**Don't ask for too much detail** — IOIs are non-binding and early. You want enough to rank and select, not a full proposal.

### Evaluating IOIs
Rank on:
1. **Price and structure** (normalized — see MA-Cheat-Sheet.md Section 1)
2. **Likelihood to close** (is this buyer real? Do they have capital? Have they done M&A before?)
3. **Strategic fit** (post-close, what happens to product, team, customers?)
4. **Speed** (timeline and ability to move)
5. **Cultural alignment** (founder/team considerations)

### Advancing to Second Round
- Select 2–4 buyers for second round (if you have that many)
- **Do not tell anyone who else is in the process by name** — just confirm it's competitive
- Send a second-round process letter outlining: what access they'll get, what they need to submit, by when

## Phase 5: Second Round — Full Diligence & Final Bids

### What Second Round Looks Like
- Full data room access (still gated — release in tranches as questions are answered)
- Management presentations (schedule separately with each buyer)
- Detailed Q&A
- Site visits (if relevant)
- Final bid submission with: price, structure, markup of any draft APA/SPA, key conditions, exclusivity period requested, planned timeline

### Management Presentation Tips
- **Prepare for hard questions:**
  - Why are you selling now? (Have a clean answer)
  - What's your best quarter's churn and why?
  - Why haven't you grown faster?
  - What would you do with more capital if you weren't selling?
  - What keeps you up at night about this business?
- **Don't overpromise** — projections you can't defend will come back to haunt in earnout negotiations
- **Let the story breathe** — buyers want to get excited. Give them room.

### Evaluating Final Bids
Same framework as IOIs but now you have much more information. Key new considerations:
- Has their view of value changed from IOI to final bid? (Re-trading is a yellow flag)
- Quality of their diligence questions — do they understand the business?
- How they treated your team during the process — signals how they'll behave post-close

## Phase 6: Term Sheet — The Most Leveraged Moment

**This is the most important phase.** You have maximum leverage before exclusivity and almost none after.

### Negotiating the TS
See MA-Cheat-Sheet.md for full term-by-term breakdown. Key principles:

- **Get the economics locked down before exclusivity.** Price, structure, escrow, earnout (if any) should all be agreed in the TS.
- **Don't leave things for "definitive docs."** Lawyers will disagree on anything left ambiguous. The seller usually loses these arguments.
- **Use competitive tension.** Even if you have a preferred buyer, let the process imply competition exists. "We're still evaluating options" is always true.
- **Don't be adversarial.** The goal is a deal that closes. Push hard on the things that matter; let go of the things that don't.

### The Exclusivity Decision
Before signing exclusivity, confirm:
- [ ] Price and structure agreed (or at least range)
- [ ] Escrow amount and duration agreed in principle
- [ ] Earnout terms (if any) clearly defined
- [ ] Key employee treatment discussed
- [ ] No major open issues that could blow up later
- [ ] Exclusivity period is reasonable (45 days preferred)
- [ ] Buyer has confirmed financing or access to capital

If major items are still open, push to resolve them before exclusivity — not after.

## Phase 7: Diligence — Managing the Process

Once exclusivity is signed, speed matters. Deals die from fatigue and delay.

### Managing the Data Room
- Use a platform (Datasite, Ansarada, Intralinks, or even Google Drive for smaller deals)
- Track what's been provided vs. outstanding
- Release materials in tranches — don't dump everything at once
- Flag anything with issues proactively: "Here's the contract, note that Section 7 has X — here's the context." Framing issues beats having buyers discover them.

### The Diligence Tracker
Critical document. Tracks every question/request from buyers, assigned owner, status, and due date. Without it:
- Things fall through the cracks
- The founder is getting pinged from all directions
- Buyer's perception: this company is a mess

**Ilene's role:** Own this document. Shield the founder from noise; route only what needs their attention.

### Keeping Momentum
Deals die when they slow down. Signs of dangerous slowdown:
- Buyer going quiet for >1 week
- Diligence requests getting increasingly nitpicky (they're building a case to re-trade)
- Buyer keeps adding people to the process without clear roles
- Counsel stops responding to redlines

**What to do:** Regular check-in cadence (at least weekly call with buyer's deal lead), clear milestone dates, and willingness to call it out if something feels off.

### Re-Trades
A re-trade = buyer comes back after diligence and says "we found something, we need to adjust the price/terms."

**When re-trades are legitimate:** Material undisclosed issues found in diligence (financial irregularities, customer losses, legal problems)

**When they're not:** Buyer using minor issues as an excuse to chip away. This is common — buyers know sellers are emotionally committed after 60 days of process.

**How to handle:** Evaluate the issue on its merits. Don't accept re-trades out of deal fatigue. Know your walk-away point. If you have other bidders (even if not in active process), remind the buyer they're not the only option.

## Phase 8: Definitive Docs & Closing

### Key Documents
- **APA (Asset Purchase Agreement)** — buyer acquires specific assets and liabilities
- **SPA (Stock Purchase Agreement)** — buyer acquires shares of the company
- **Merger Agreement** — for formal mergers; more common at larger deal sizes
- **Disclosure Schedules** — attached to the APA/SPA; lists all exceptions to reps and warranties. This is where the devil lives.
- **Employment Agreements** — for founders and key employees
- **IP Assignment Agreements** — confirming IP transfers cleanly
- **Officer's Certificate** — certifying reps and warranties are true at close

### Disclosure Schedules
Often more important than the main agreement. Every exception to a rep and warranty must be listed here — if it's not listed, seller is liable.

Common mistakes:
- Being too narrow in what you disclose (leaves out minor issues that become claims later)
- Being too broad (generic disclosures that the buyer's counsel will reject)
- Rushing them — they're drafted by lawyers but need business team input on every item

### Conditions to Closing Checklist
- [ ] All board/shareholder approvals obtained
- [ ] Required regulatory filings (HSR if above thresholds, state filings)
- [ ] Required third-party consents (customer contracts, key vendor agreements)
- [ ] Key employee offer letters signed
- [ ] Financing confirmed (if buyer used debt/equity for the acquisition)
- [ ] No material adverse change since signing

### Employee Communication Timing
One of the hardest judgment calls in a deal. General guidance:
- **Before signing:** Tell only the people who absolutely need to know (often just CEO and 1–2 key team members). Leaks kill deals.
- **At signing (before close):** For key employees who need to sign agreements, tell them. For broad team, consider timing.
- **At close or right before:** Broad team announcement. Don't let people find out from a news article.
- **Message matters:** "This is a great outcome for the team" needs to be true and demonstrable, not spin.

## Common Pitfalls — Quick Reference

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Going to market before ready | Urgency, impatience | Run pre-diligence audit first |
| Only one buyer in the process | Inbound feels like enough | Always try to create competition |
| Losing leverage before exclusivity | Eager to close | Lock economic terms before signing exclusivity |
| Data room surprises | Didn't pre-audit | Know your own issues; frame them proactively |
| Deal fatigue → accepting re-trades | Process drags on | Set milestone dates; maintain urgency |
| Board misalignment mid-process | Didn't align early | Align before launch, not during |
| Bad legal counsel | Didn't vet lawyers | Confirm M&A track record before engaging |
| Employee leaks | Too many people know | Need-to-know basis until close |
| Earnout agreed without protections | Didn't know to ask | See MA-Cheat-Sheet.md Section 3 |
